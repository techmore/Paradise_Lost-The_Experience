About John Milton

John Milton (1608–1674) was an English poet, polemicist, and civil servant who served as Secretary for Foreign Tongues under Oliver Cromwell. He was a man of prodigious learning—fluent in Latin, Greek, Hebrew, French, Spanish, and Italian—whose political and religious convictions shaped his life's work.

By 1652, Milton had gone completely blind. Paradise Lost was composed entirely through dictation to a series of amanuenses, including his daughters and friends. The poem represents the culmination of a lifetime of reading, reflection, and political engagement, synthesizing classical epic tradition, biblical theology, and the turbulent politics of 17th-century England.

Milton's body of work includes the early poems L'Allegro and Il Penseroso, the masque Comus, the elegy Lycidas, and the later epics Paradise Regained and the tragedy Samson Agonistes. But Paradise Lost remains his magnum opus—a work he described as "things unattempted yet in prose or rhyme."

The Poem's Structure

Paradise Lost was first published in 1667 in ten books. In 1674, Milton reorganized it into twelve books (following the Virgilian model) with minimal textual changes. This site presents the original ten-book edition.

The poem follows a tripartite structure:

  • Books I–IV: The Fallen World — Satan's rebellion, the war in Heaven, the fall of the rebel angels, and the consultation in Hell. Satan's journey to Earth and his first sight of Adam and Eve.
  • Books V–VIII: The Backstory — Raphael's visit to Eden, the War in Heaven (narrated as a warning), and the Creation of the World.
  • Books IX–X: The Fall — The temptation and fall of Adam and Eve, the immediate consequences, and the Son's judgment. Michael shows Adam the future of humanity.

The poem opens in medias res (in the middle of things), with Satan and his legions already cast into Hell. The epic conventions Milton employs—the invocation of the Muse, the council scene, the epic catalogue, the descent to the underworld—all deliberately echo Homer and Virgil while redirecting classical forms to Christian subject matter.

Key Themes

Free Will & Obedience

Central to the poem's theology is the idea that humanity was created "sufficient to have stood, though free to fall." Milton's God does not create puppets but beings capable of genuine love and genuine rebellion.

Pride & Ambition

Satan's "sense of injured merit" and refusal to serve spark the cosmic rebellion. The poem examines how ambition untethered from humility corrupts even the brightest of beings.

Temptation & Deception

The serpent's seduction of Eve is a masterclass in rhetorical manipulation—flattery, half-truths, and the promise of godlike knowledge. The Fall comes not through force but through persuasion.

Justice & Mercy

The Father's justice and the Son's voluntary sacrifice establish the poem's moral framework. The "fortunate fall" (felix culpa) tradition suggests that the Fall ultimately leads to a greater good through redemption.

Hierarchy & Order

Milton's universe is hierarchically ordered: God, angels, humans, animals, matter. Disruption of this chain—whether by Satan's rebellion or Eve's transgression—results in cosmic disorder.

Knowledge & Innocence

The tree of knowledge represents not learning itself but the knowledge of evil gained through disobedience. Milton explores what it means to know good only through the experience of its opposite.

Historical Context

Paradise Lost was composed during and after one of the most turbulent periods in English history. The English Civil War (1642–1651), the execution of Charles I (1649), the Interregnum under Cromwell, and the Restoration of Charles II (1660) form the political backdrop of the poem—particularly its exploration of rebellion, authority, and legitimate rule.

Milton was deeply engaged in the political and religious debates of his time. His prose works on divorce, press freedom (Areopagitica), and republican government all inform the poem's concerns with liberty, tyranny, and the nature of just authority. The poem can be read as a meditation on the failure of the English Republic and God's mysterious providence working through human history.

Milton's Theology

Milton was a heterodox Christian whose views departed from mainstream Puritanism in several important respects. He was an anti-Trinitarian (Arian), believing the Son was subordinate to the Father and not co-eternal or consubstantial. He believed in mortalism (the soul sleeps at death until the resurrection) and rejected predestination in favor of free will.

The poem's God is not the inscrutable deity of Calvinist theology but a being who explicitly defends his justice and explains his purposes. The Arminian emphasis on free will—the idea that humans can resist or accept grace—is central to the poem's dramatic tension and moral vision.

Critical Debates

Most modern Paradise Lost reading gets shaped by a small group of books that argue with one another. Reading them together is often more useful than reading any one in isolation.

C.S. Lewis, A Preface to Paradise Lost

The classic defense of Milton's poem. Lewis treats Satan as a great rhetorical power, but not a figure to admire.

William Empson, Milton's God

The famous contrarian response. Empson presses on the poem's moral and theological tensions, especially around divine justice.

Stanley Fish, Surprised by Sin

A landmark reader-response account. Fish argues the poem shapes and corrects our sympathies as we move through it.

A.J.A. Waldock, Paradise Lost and Its Critics

An earlier skeptical reading that helps frame the later debate around Milton's theology and narrative authority.

Helen Gardner, A Reading of Paradise Lost

A thoughtful middle path, often useful when the Lewis-Empson-Fish debate starts to feel too polarized.

John Leonard, Faithful Labourers

A major history of Paradise Lost criticism, tracing how the poem has been read from 1667 into the twentieth century.

Textual Editions

For a denser scholarly apparatus, the standard print editions are worth knowing even if you read online.

  • Alastair Fowler's Longman edition is the most heavily cited academic edition and remains a gold standard for notes and commentary.
  • Gordon Teskey's Norton Critical Edition is a strong alternative if you want key essays bundled with the poem.

Background Texts

These help show what Milton is rewriting, arguing with, or echoing across the poem.

  • Genesis 1–3 for the basic narrative frame
  • Augustine's City of God for the theodicy problem
  • Virgil's Aeneid for epic inheritance and revision
  • Spenser's The Faerie Queene for Milton's English poetic ancestry

Milton's Prose

Milton's own prose is often the best background reading for the poem's politics and theology.

  • Areopagitica on free expression and censorship
  • The divorce tracts for Milton's thinking about marriage and conscience
  • De Doctrina Christiana for his heterodox theology

Biography and Context

  • Barbara Lewalski, The Life of John Milton for the standard modern biography
  • Christopher Hill, Milton and the English Revolution for the political reading
  • BBC In Our Time for a compact audio primer on the poem and its debates

Getting the Most Out of Paradise Lost

Paradise Lost repays slow, attentive reading. Here are some approaches to deepen your engagement:

  • Read aloud. Milton's blank verse is meant to be heard. The rhythm, enjambment, and sonic texture come alive when spoken.
  • Follow the syntax. Milton's sentences are often long and complex. Pay attention to how clauses unfold across multiple lines.
  • Track the similes. Milton's epic similes are miniature poems in themselves—each one rewards unpacking.
  • Note biblical echoes. The poem is saturated with Scripture. A Bible with cross-references is an invaluable companion.
  • Consider Satan's rhetoric. The most beautiful speeches in the poem often belong to the devil. This is intentional—Milton shows how evil persuades.
  • Read with others. Paradise Lost was written to be discussed. A reading group or online discussion can transform the experience.
