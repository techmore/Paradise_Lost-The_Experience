Scholarly editions, critical studies, and background texts available for download. Each work includes a purchase link to the published edition.

A Preface to Paradise Lost

C. S. Lewis

The classic defense of Milton's poem. Lewis treats Satan as a great rhetorical power—but not a figure to admire—and reads the poem through the lens of epic convention and Christian orthodoxy.

City of God

St. Augustine

Augustine's magnum opus, foundational to the theodicy problem Milton confronts in Paradise Lost. Essential background for understanding the poem's engagement with divine justice, the nature of evil, and the shape of history.

Milton: Paradise Lost

John Milton, ed. Alastair Fowler

The Longman Annotated English Poets edition—the most heavily cited academic edition of Paradise Lost. Fowler's notes and commentary represent the gold standard for scholarly close reading of the poem.

Milton's God

William Empson

The famous contrarian response to the Lewis tradition. Empson presses hard on the poem's moral and theological tensions, arguing that Milton's God is the real problem—and that the poem's power comes from its unresolved conflicts.

Paradise Lost

John Milton, ed. Gordon Teskey

The Norton Critical Edition (Second International Student Edition). A strong alternative to Fowler, bundling the poem with key critical essays, contextual documents, and thorough editorial apparatus.

Paradise Lost and Its Critics

A. J. A. Waldock

An early skeptical reading of Paradise Lost that helped frame the modern critical debate. Waldock challenges the poem's narrative coherence and questions whether Milton's stated theological aims match what the poem actually achieves.

Surprised by Sin: The Reader in Paradise Lost

Stanley Fish

A landmark reader-response account. Fish argues that Paradise Lost is designed to trap and then correct the reader's sympathies—especially toward Satan—making the experience of reading the poem itself the site of the Fall and redemption.
